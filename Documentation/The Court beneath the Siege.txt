THE COURT BENEATH THE SIEGE
Core plot proposal and first implemented conversation pass

The siege should force private obligations into public view. Each companion needs something that can be defended honestly, something they would rather conceal, and another person whose judgement they cannot simply dismiss. The Emperor's qualities should change what he notices and how he can respond, rather than turn every conversation into a contest he wins by selecting the highest number. These are original fictional plots; the historical characters' private conduct is dramatic invention.

The first web is a ledger of people. Giovanni's company still owes wages to dead men's households. Notaras has prepared travel chests for his own family and the public archives. Sphrantzes has quietly kept a ransom list that includes his children. Diedo must choose who gets passage on a crowded dispatch boat; Barbaro knows the shipmaster and has an undeclared financial interest. These stories meet over a single question: may the people defending the City make provision for those they love, and who must be told? There should be no secret route that manufactures extra seats or erases the bill. A defensible settlement leaves a name, a witness and somebody disappointed.

The second web runs beneath the stones. Grant's inspection discovers a refuge omitted from the food lists. Cyriacus recognises its ancient benefactor in an inscription the masons want to reuse. Isidore has arranged relief for households hostile to his Church policy, concealing his authorship to protect the recipients. Orhan has allowed family letters through the lines; Kjold needs to know who can approach his sentries. These plots should share witnesses and disputed passages without making every secret part of one master conspiracy. Investigating one person may give the Emperor a fairer question to ask another. Evidence of private correspondence is not automatically evidence of treachery.

The third web is the price of appearing competent. Orban accepted doubtful metal to outpace a rival. Fontana wants another trial badly enough to consider a patron's request to suppress a failure. Stephen has let his soldiers overestimate his experience; Kjold can correct him without humiliating him. Bertrandon's apparently certain road comes from an old memory and an unacknowledged guide. Tafur discovers that the household he praised in his travels is poorer and more complicated than his account. Their quests ask whether an Emperor can preserve someone's usefulness without preserving their deception. The useful conversation is the one after the impressive story begins to fail.

The writing review applies four tests: historical fiction's uncomfortable private motive; philosophical satire's precise institutional absurdity; suspense grounded in ordinary objects and incomplete knowledge; and consequential RPG quests whose people continue to want things after the player's answer. This is an editorial exercise inspired by those traditions, not a claim that deceased writers reviewed the game. Cut interchangeable wisdom, reward captions disguised as dialogue and characters who agree because the player clicked kindly. A borrowed banner, a clean sword, a chest and a forged wage entry should carry more weight than another speech about destiny.

The first implementation supplies fifteen three-stage court threads, dated decisions, disclosed secrets, expressed affinities and tensions, stat requirements, cross-character information routes and ending recollections. They presently resolve through conversation and recorded arrangements: they do not secretly spawn supplies, move armies, grant passage or adjudicate the separate murder case. Extending those settlements into military or resource systems is a further design decision. Next, test whether players remember whom they disappointed and why. That is a better test of the court than whether they exhaust every golden dialogue marker.
